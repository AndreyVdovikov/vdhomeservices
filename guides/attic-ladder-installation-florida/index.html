Attic ladder installation: what to know before you cut

Ceiling height, the opening, trusses and the order of the work. What a folding attic ladder really needs, and where a handyman job ends and a call to an engineer begins.

By Andrew · Updated November 18, 2026

A folding attic ladder turns a hard-to-reach attic into usable storage, and it is a job people often try on their own. It can go well, but it is easy to get wrong: a ladder that is not fastened into the framing, or legs trimmed to the wrong length, is a fall waiting to happen. This guide explains how the installation works, what to measure first, the Florida detail that catches many people, and when to hand the job to someone else.

Do you need one

If you store things in the attic, or need to get to the A/C air handler, the water heater, wiring or insulation, a proper ladder is far safer than a stepladder balanced next to a shelf of boxes. If you only go up once a year, a stepladder and a small access hatch may be enough, but anyone who uses the attic regularly will be glad to have a real ladder.

Measure before you buy

Three numbers decide whether a ladder fits your house. The first is the ceiling height next to the opening, measured from the floor. Most aluminum folding ladders cover about 7'8" to 10'3", and the legs are trimmed to fit. The second is the clear floor space from the opening to the foot of the ladder, about 67" for a common size, with no door swing or wall in the way. The third is the space the sections need while they unfold, around 74". Your model's manual gives its exact numbers.

Think about the attic side too. The space above the opening needs to be clear, with room to step off, and the ladder should not land where it blocks a hallway or a door.

The rough opening

The opening is the framed hole in the ceiling. A common size is 22.5" by 54", which fits between joists spaced 24" apart, so nothing has to be cut. Some models are a little wider. It runs along the joists, with a header closing each end, and it has to be square: the two diagonals should match within 1/8", or the ladder will bind and the lid will not close properly.

Florida trusses

Most newer Florida homes, including the ones in Wesley Chapel, are built with roof trusses and not with separate ceiling joists. The bottom chord of a truss is part of the structure holding the roof together, and it must not be cut without an engineer's approval. If the opening fits between two trusses without cutting anything, the ladder can usually go there. If the only way is to cut a chord, stop, because that is a job for a licensed contractor and an engineer and not for a handyman. The same goes for old-style joists: if one has to be removed, it needs temporary support, doubled headers and a proper structural fix first, never a casual cut. When I look at an attic ladder job, finding out what is above the ceiling is the first thing I do.

How the installation goes

These are the main stages. Follow your own model's manual for the exact order, hole sizes and hardware, because they differ. The work always needs two people, one below and one in the attic, since the ladder is heavy and awkward to lift into the opening, and the person in the attic cannot come down until the ladder is fastened, so the tools and a flashlight go up first.

It starts on the floor, where the ladder gets its bushings and metal straps, and the lid and sections stay closed and tied, because the lid is under spring tension. Boards screwed into the joists form temporary supports, and the ladder is lifted into the opening and set on them with the hinge side tight against the header and centered. Shims go in on both sides, and now the diagonals are measured and the lid is checked to sit in the frame without catching. Then the frame is fastened with the lag screws supplied with the ladder, never with drywall or deck screws, into the framing as the manual shows. After that the temporary supports come off, the lid is tested several times with the pull cord in place, and the metal straps are cut. Only then is the ladder unfolded and the legs trimmed. The finish is cutting the extra shims, adding casing around the opening and tightening the hardware after the first few days of use.

Trimming the legs

This is the step people worry about, with good reason. The ladder is made longer than most ceilings need, and the legs are cut to fit your height. Your manual has a chart that matches your ceiling height to where to cut the side rails and whether the adjustable feet need trimming. Measure the ceiling height at the opening, write it down, and read the chart twice before cutting. Cut one side rail, compare it with the other, then cut the second the same, and take off the burrs. The adjustable feet go on so that the whole rubber heel sits flat on the floor. Then check the joints: the sections must close tightly, and both feet must touch the floor. A gap at a joint, whether the ladder is too short or too long, makes it unsafe, and it has to be fixed before anyone climbs.

Safety

Do not open the lid or cut the straps on the sections until the manual says so, because the spring tension is strong. Do not climb the ladder until every lag screw is in and the legs are trimmed. Do not over-tighten the hardware either, since an aluminum frame can bend and then the lid will not close. Keep the lid free of anything resting on it and the floor below clear, check the hardware again after the first few days, and keep to the load rating printed on the ladder. A little silicone spray on the hinges and arms now and then keeps it working smoothly.

DIY or hire a pro

Installing a ladder into an existing, correctly framed opening is a reasonable job for two handy people who read the manual carefully. I would call someone when you want it done quickly and cleanly, when the ceiling is high, when the attic access is awkward, or when you are not sure what is above the ceiling. I install folding attic ladders and do the finishing work around them. FAQ

How much ceiling height does a folding attic ladder need?

Most aluminum folding ladders fit ceilings from about 7'8" to 10'3", and the legs are trimmed to your exact height. Check the range printed for your model before you buy.

How big is the opening for an attic ladder?

A common size is 22.5 by 54 inches, which fits between joists that are 24 inches apart. Some models use a wider opening, such as 25.5 by 54 inches. Always use the size from your ladder's manual.

Can I put an attic ladder in a ceiling with trusses?

Many Florida homes have roof trusses. The bottom chord of a truss must not be cut without an engineer's approval. If your opening fits between trusses without cutting anything, it can work. If framing has to be cut, call a licensed contractor or an engineer first.

Do I need two people to install an attic ladder?

Yes. One person works from below and one from the attic, and the ladder is heavy and awkward to lift into the opening. Do not try it alone.

Why do the legs have to be trimmed?

Folding ladders come longer than most ceilings. The legs are cut to your ceiling height so the ladder sits flat on the floor and the sections close tight at the joints. A gap at a joint means the ladder is not safe to use.

How long does the job take?

With a clear attic and a ready opening, usually a few hours. Making a new opening, framing and finishing the trim takes longer.

Do you install attic ladders?

I install folding attic ladders in existing openings and help with the finishing work around them. If the job needs cutting trusses or structural changes, I will tell you and send you to a licensed contractor.
