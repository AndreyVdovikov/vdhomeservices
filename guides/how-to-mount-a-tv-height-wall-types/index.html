How to mount a TV: height, wall types and cables

The right height, the right mount and the right anchors for your wall, with the Florida-specific details most guides miss: block walls, lanais, bright rooms and humidity.

By Andrew · Updated November 15, 2026

Mounting a TV looks easy until you are standing in front of the wall with a drill and no idea whether the mount is going to hold. I mount TVs for homeowners in Wesley Chapel and across Tampa Bay every week, on drywall, block, brick, tile and lanai walls. This is the guide I would give a friend: how high to hang it, which mount to buy, what to do about your wall, and how to hide the cables.

How high to mount a TV

The rule that works for most living rooms: the center of the screen at eye level when you are seated. For most sofas and chairs, that is about 40 to 42 inches from the floor to the center of the TV. A few details:

  • Measure to the center of the screen, not the top or the bottom. Different TVs have different frames.
  • Sit where you normally sit and have someone hold a piece of painter's tape on the wall at your eye level. That is your center line.
  • If you mostly watch lying down in bed, mount higher, about 50 inches to the center, or use a tilting mount.
  • Leave a few inches between the bottom of the TV and any furniture or media console below it.
  • A TV mounted too high makes your neck work. If you cannot avoid a high position, use a tilting mount so the screen faces down toward you.

TV size and viewing distance

A simple guide for the right TV size is to divide your seating distance (in inches) by about 1.6. Here is a quick reference:

Sitting distanceComfortable TV size
5 to 6 feet43 to 50 inches
6 to 8 feet55 to 65 inches
8 to 10 feet65 to 75 inches
10 to 12 feet75 to 85 inches
12 feet and more85 inches and larger

It also helps to keep the viewing angle in mind. If you sit far off to the side, the picture gets worse on many TVs, and a swivel mount can turn the screen toward you. The vertical angle matters as well: the less you tilt your head up, the better.

Which mount to use

  • Fixed mount. The slimmest and cheapest. The TV sits flat against the wall. Best when the TV is at the right height and you do not need to adjust it.
  • Tilting mount. The screen angles down. Good for TVs mounted a bit higher, such as over a console, or in rooms with glare.
  • Full-motion (articulating) mount. The TV pulls out, swivels and tilts. Good for corners, open rooms and when you watch from different spots. It is heavier and needs a solid anchor, and I always mount it into studs or solid backing.

Before you buy, check three things: the mount supports your TV's weight, it fits the VESA pattern (the hole spacing on the back of the TV) and it is rated for your TV size. All of these are on the mount's box and on the TV's spec sheet.

Your wall type

Drywall over wood studs

The most common interior wall. The mount must go into the studs, usually spaced 16 inches apart, not just into the drywall. A stud finder tells you where they are. Drywall alone cannot hold a TV, even with heavy-duty anchors, once the TV is large or the mount extends from the wall.

If the studs are not where you want the TV, there are two good answers: a wide mount plate that spreads over two studs, or horizontal blocking added inside the wall.

Concrete block and masonry

Very common in Florida homes, especially for exterior walls and garages. It needs a hammer drill, masonry bits and the right masonry anchors, set into the solid block and not into the hollow joints. It is a solid base once done right.

Brick and stone

Similar to block, with extra care. The drilling needs to go into the brick or the solid stone, with the correct anchors, and the pattern of mortar joints affects where the holes can go.

Tile

Mounting on tile means drilling through it without cracking it, with the right bit, slow speed and a careful layout. Ideally the holes land in the center of a tile and not on the edge.

Plaster and stucco

Older plaster walls and stucco exteriors need to be treated like masonry, depending on what is behind them. The type of backing decides the right anchor.

Over a fireplace

A TV over the fireplace is a popular look, but it is not always a good idea. Two questions decide it. First, heat: a wood-burning or gas fireplace can damage the TV, so always follow the manufacturer's clearances. An electric fireplace gives off much less heat, but you still need to check the manuals for both. Second, height: a TV over a mantel often ends up too high for comfortable viewing. A tilting mount helps, and so does a media wall that sets the TV lower and puts the fireplace beneath it. You can see examples of this on the media wall page.

Hiding the cables

Dangling cables spoil a clean mount. Your options are:

  • Paintable cord cover (raceway). A slim channel that sticks to the wall and runs down to the console. It is quick, tidy and painted to match your wall.
  • In-wall rated cables. Where the wall has an open path, low-voltage cables (HDMI, for example) can be run inside the wall. Use cables that are rated for in-wall use.
  • Power outlet behind the TV. Power cords must not be extended inside the wall. If you want the outlet behind the TV, this is a job for a licensed electrician. I do not do electrical wiring, but I plan the mounting around it.

Not every wall allows a hidden run, for example when there is block behind the surface. In that case, the cord cover is the clean solution.

Florida things to know

  • Block walls. Many houses have block exterior walls, which need masonry anchors. Do not use drywall anchors on block.
  • Sunlight and glare. Bright Florida rooms can wash out the picture. A tilting or swivel mount helps you aim the screen away from the glare, and the TV position matters before drilling.
  • Lanais and patios. A TV outside needs to be outdoor-rated for heat, humidity and moisture, and the mount should be suitable for outdoor use. A covered lanai is the best place.
  • Humidity. Moisture is hard on electronics and on rusty hardware. Use quality stainless or coated hardware in outdoor and humid areas.

Before you start

  1. Decide the TV position from your seat, with tape on the wall.
  2. Find out your wall type and where the studs are.
  3. Check the TV weight, size and VESA pattern, and buy the matching mount.
  4. Plan how the cables and power will run.
  5. Get a second person to hold the TV when you lift it.
  6. Check level at every step before the final tightening.

Common mistakes

  • Mounting too high. The most frequent mistake, especially over a console or fireplace.
  • Missing the studs. A mount in drywall alone can pull out of the wall.
  • Using the wrong anchor for the wall. Drywall anchors in block, or the wrong masonry anchor in brick.
  • Choosing the wrong mount. A fixed mount where you need to tilt, or a mount rated below the TV's weight.
  • Forgetting the cables. No plan for power and cables, and a messy result after all the work.
  • Not checking level before tightening. A TV that is off by a degree looks wrong all the time.

DIY or hire someone

A small TV on a drywall wall with studs where you want them is a fair DIY job if you have a stud finder, a level and a second pair of hands. I would hire someone when the TV is large and heavy (the bigger it gets, the higher the risk), the wall is block, brick or tile, you want the cables hidden, or you want it done once and done right. FAQ

How high should I mount my TV?

As a starting point, the center of the screen should be at about eye level when you are seated, which is roughly 40 to 42 inches from the floor for most living rooms. In a bedroom, where you often watch lying down, mount it a bit higher.

Can I mount a TV on a drywall wall without a stud?

For anything but a very small TV, no. The mount should go into studs or into solid blocking behind the drywall. If studs are not where you want the TV, blocking can be added inside the wall, or a mount that spreads across two studs can be used.

How do you mount a TV on a block or concrete wall?

With masonry anchors and a hammer drill, drilling into the solid block and not the hollow joints. Many Florida homes have block exterior walls, so this is common, and it needs the right anchors and care.

Can you hide the cables?

Yes. The tidy options are a paintable cord cover on the wall or, where the wall allows it, in-wall rated cables. A power outlet behind the TV is a licensed electrician's job, and I can plan the mounting around it.

Can I mount a TV above a fireplace?

Only if the fireplace gives off little heat and the manufacturer's clearance allows it. Always check the TV and fireplace manuals. A TV mounted too high above a fireplace is also uncomfortable to watch.

What size TV should I get for my room?

A common rule is to divide your viewing distance in inches by about 1.6 for a comfortable size. For example, sitting 8 feet (96 inches) away points to roughly a 60 inch TV.

Do you mount TVs on lanais and outdoors?

Yes, on the wall. Outdoor TVs should be outdoor-rated for heat, humidity and moisture, and the mount needs to be suitable for outdoor use. A covered lanai is the best location.
