How to mount a TV: height, wall types and cables

The right height, the right mount and the right anchors for your wall, with the Florida details most guides miss: block walls, lanais and bright rooms.

By Andrew · Updated November 15, 2026

Mounting a TV looks easy until you are standing in front of the wall with a drill and no idea whether the mount is going to hold. I mount TVs for homeowners in Wesley Chapel and across Tampa Bay every week, on drywall, block, brick, tile and lanai walls, so this is the guide I would give a friend: how high to hang it, which mount to buy, what to do about your wall, and how to deal with the cables.

How high to mount a TV

The rule that works for most living rooms is to put the center of the screen at eye level when you are seated. For most sofas that means about 40 to 42 inches from the floor to the center of the TV. Measure to the center of the screen and not the top, because TVs have different frames. The easiest way to find the spot is to sit where you normally sit and have someone put a piece of painter's tape on the wall at your eye level. That tape is your center line.

Bedrooms are different, since you often watch lying down, so mount the TV higher there, around 50 inches to the center, or use a tilting mount. Leave a few inches between the bottom of the TV and any console below it. A TV that is mounted too high makes your neck do the work, and if you cannot avoid a high position, a tilting mount lets the screen face down toward you.

TV size and distance

A simple way to pick the size is to divide your seating distance in inches by about 1.6. Sitting five or six feet away, a 43 to 50 inch TV is comfortable. From six to eight feet, think 55 to 65 inches. At eight to ten feet, 65 to 75 inches works, and above ten feet you can go to 75 inches and bigger. The viewing angle matters as well. If you sit far off to the side, many TVs look worse, and a swivel mount can turn the screen toward you.

Which mount to use

A fixed mount is the slimmest and cheapest, and it keeps the TV flat against the wall, so it is right when the TV is at the correct height and you do not need to move it. A tilting mount lets the screen angle down, which helps when the TV sits a bit higher than ideal, for example over a console, or in a room with glare. A full-motion mount pulls the TV out, swivels it and tilts it, which is great for corners and open rooms, but it is heavier and needs a solid anchor, so I always mount those into studs or solid backing.

Before buying, check three things on the mount's box and on your TV's spec sheet: the mount supports your TV's weight, it fits the VESA pattern (the hole spacing on the back of the TV), and it is rated for your TV's size.

Your wall type

Most interior walls are drywall over wood studs, and the mount has to go into the studs, usually spaced 16 inches apart, not just into the drywall. A stud finder tells you where they are. Drywall alone cannot hold a large TV, even with heavy-duty anchors. If the studs are not where you want the TV, there are two good answers: a wide mount plate that spans two studs, or blocking added inside the wall.

Concrete block is very common in Florida, especially on exterior walls and in garages. It needs a hammer drill, masonry bits and masonry anchors set into the solid block, not the hollow joints, and once it is done right it is a very solid base. Brick and stone work the same way, with extra care about where the mortar joints fall. Tile means drilling through it without cracking it, with the right bit, a slow speed and a careful layout so the holes land in the middle of a tile, not on an edge. Older plaster and stucco walls are treated like masonry, depending on what is behind them.

Over a fireplace

A TV over the fireplace is a popular look, but it is not always a good idea. The first question is heat. A wood-burning or gas fireplace can damage a TV, so always follow the manufacturer's clearances. An electric fireplace gives off much less heat, but check both manuals anyway. The second question is height, because a TV over a mantel often ends up too high for comfortable viewing. A tilting mount helps, and so does a custom media wall that sets the TV lower and puts the fireplace beneath it.

Hiding the cables

Dangling cables spoil a clean mount. The quickest tidy fix is a paintable cord cover, a slim channel that sticks to the wall and runs down to the console, painted to match. Where the wall has an open path, low-voltage cables such as HDMI can run inside the wall, as long as they are rated for in-wall use. Power is a different matter: cords must not be extended inside the wall, and an outlet behind the TV is a job for a licensed electrician. I do not do electrical wiring, but I plan the mounting around it. Not every wall allows a hidden run, for example when there is block behind the surface, and in that case the cord cover is the clean solution.

Florida things to know

Block exterior walls are everywhere here, and they need masonry anchors, never drywall anchors. Bright rooms with big windows can wash out the picture, so a tilting or swivel mount helps you aim the screen away from the glare, and it is worth deciding the TV position before drilling. A TV outdoors, on a lanai or patio, has to be outdoor-rated for heat, humidity and moisture, with a mount that is suitable for outdoor use, and a covered lanai is the best place. In humid spots I use stainless or coated hardware, because ordinary hardware rusts.

Common mistakes

The most frequent mistake is mounting the TV too high, especially over a console or a fireplace. Close behind is missing the studs and relying on drywall alone, which can pull out of the wall. People also use the wrong anchor for the wall, such as drywall anchors in block, or buy a fixed mount where they need a tilt, or a mount rated below the TV's weight. Many forget to plan the cables and power until the end, which gives a messy result after all the work. And skipping the level check before the final tightening leaves a TV that is a degree off and looks wrong every day.

DIY or hire someone

A small TV on a drywall wall, with studs where you want them, is a fair DIY job if you have a stud finder, a level and a second pair of hands. I would hire someone when the TV is large and heavy, the wall is block, brick or tile, you want the cables hidden, or you simply want it done once and done right. FAQ

How high should I mount my TV?

As a starting point, the center of the screen should be at about eye level when you are seated, which is roughly 40 to 42 inches from the floor for most living rooms. In a bedroom, where you often watch lying down, mount it a bit higher.

Can I mount a TV on a drywall wall without a stud?

For anything but a very small TV, no. The mount should go into studs or into solid blocking behind the drywall. If studs are not where you want the TV, blocking can be added inside the wall, or a mount that spreads across two studs can be used.

How do you mount a TV on a block or concrete wall?

With masonry anchors and a hammer drill, drilling into the solid block and not the hollow joints. Many Florida homes have block exterior walls, so this is common, and it needs the right anchors and care.

Can you hide the cables?

Yes. The tidy options are a paintable cord cover on the wall or, where the wall allows it, in-wall rated cables. A power outlet behind the TV is a licensed electrician's job, and I can plan the mounting around it.

Can I mount a TV above a fireplace?

Only if the fireplace gives off little heat and the manufacturer's clearance allows it. Always check the TV and fireplace manuals. A TV mounted too high above a fireplace is also uncomfortable to watch.

What size TV should I get for my room?

A common rule is to divide your viewing distance in inches by about 1.6 for a comfortable size. For example, sitting 8 feet (96 inches) away points to roughly a 60 inch TV.

Do you mount TVs on lanais and outdoors?

Yes, on the wall. Outdoor TVs should be outdoor-rated for heat, humidity and moisture, and the mount needs to be suitable for outdoor use. A covered lanai is the best location.
